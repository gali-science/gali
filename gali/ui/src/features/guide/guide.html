Reviewing a paper with AI

Use it for

  • Equations and derivations
  • Numbers across text and tables
  • Claims against the reported results
  • References that do not exist
  • Prior work the authors missed
  • Explaining hard passages

Do not rely on it for

  • Scores or accept / reject verdicts
  • A verdict on novelty or impact
  • Proof that a paper is error-free
  • A second, independent opinion

How to work

  1. Read the paper yourself first
  2. Ask for checks
  3. Verify every flag in the paper
  4. Assume it missed something
  5. Write your own verdict

Reviewing a paper with AI

November 2026

In short

There are more papers than ever out there and it is becoming impossible to efficiently provide a timely and thorough review for all of them. To become more efficient, using AI to review papers can be very tempting. However, it is critical to approach AI-assisted reviewing in a mindful and ethical way.

What does this mean in practice? It means awareness of the latest research on capabilities and limitations of AI reviewers. It also means never giving AI the final say on anything in the paper. The decisions are yours to make.

AI is good at some tasks and still bad at others. It can help you find bugs in code and errors in formulas, but it has a harder time judging how significant or novel a paper is (to be honest, so do humans). If you ask AI whether a paper is good, it may have no reference point and just agree with whatever your question hints at.

The AI reviewer also doesn't know what a request costs. Asking for one more ablation is free for the model and might cost the authors months. The objectivity is in the numbers, but doing science is a social and subjective activity (otherwise reviewers would never disagree). Reviewing is a negotiation and AI has absolutely no idea about that.

Research also shows that what's true of AI models changes quickly, so our approach has to change too. Below are our notes on how we think AI can be used well in reviewing.

How AI can help

Writing a paper is hard and mistakes slip in. Published papers contain quite a few mistakes and we think AI is good at helping to find them: numbers in a table that don't match the text, or equations with wrong symbols.

Bianchi and colleagues ran an AI checker built on GPT-5 on 2,500 papers from ICLR, NeurIPS and TMLR.2 It looked only for mistakes with a right answer, in formulas, calculations, figures and tables. Depending on the venue, it flagged at least one potentially serious mistake in 24% to 36% of papers. The number it found per paper went up over time (Figure 1).

When experts went through 316 mistakes the checker reported, they confirmed 263 of them (about 83%) and rated 86 of those as serious. This is where AI helps most: mistakes that are objective and easy to verify.

Similar results came from Agents4Science, a 2025 conference where AI models did the first round of reviews.3 The AI reviewers caught real problems, from a mismatched statistic to an abstract that contradicted the paper's results.

Beyond catching mistakes, AI can explain things. If a derivation is dense or a method is new to you, a model can often walk you through it. Just remember that the explanation might be wrong and that the paper should make sense on its own. If you couldn't follow a passage without a model's help, that's likely something to point out in your review.

Where AI fails

We believe this is the most important part of this piece: learning where AI can fail. Figure 2 shows where we draw the line.

First of all, AI checkers can still miss mistakes, so not finding any doesn't mean the paper is perfect. More importantly, they also report mistakes that aren't there. In Bianchi's study, about one report in six was wrong, often because the paper used unusual notation or because text got garbled when the tool read the PDF.2 Every report is something to check, not something to copy into your review (Figure 3).

In addition to the above, AI review scores follow the writing. Baumann and colleagues had models rewrite 60 ICLR 2026 papers in a single automated pass, with no new experiments. The scores went up by almost half a point on a ten-point scale (Figure 4).1 Writing is an important part of science, but how much it should count is a human decision and definitely not an AI one.

AI scores also say little about quality. In the same study, AI scores agreed with each other far more than with human scores and they were much higher. And models agreeing with each other doesn't make them right. At ICLR 2026, the average human score predicted the final decision better than the average AI score.1

AI also asks for too much and asks generically. In a large survey at AAAI 2026, people judged AI reviews more likely to overemphasize minor issues.1 In Baumann's test, each AI reviewer's favorite requests, like "how sensitive is the method to the choice of [...]," showed up in 13% to 22% of papers. The most common phrases in human reviews showed up in fewer than 1%.1 Only ask for an experiment if its result could change your mind.

AI loves hedging. When models rewrote papers for higher AI scores, they added far more words like "may" and "suggests" than they took out (Figure 4). The rewritten papers also scored higher.1 A hedged claim has no more evidence behind it. The same thing can happen to a review you draft with AI.

Papers can also try to manipulate AI reviewers by hiding instructions that tell them to be positive.1 Agents4Science found two papers that tried it.3 If you run a paper through a model, you're also running whatever is hidden in it.

And AI reviews sound alike. Among real ICLR 2026 reviews, the fully AI-written ones were slightly more similar to each other than the rest, in every one of the conference's 21 research areas. In a controlled test the gap was large.1 So asking five models isn't like asking five reviewers. It's closer to asking one reviewer five times. That's one more reason to read the paper before you ask a model anything. You can, however, prompt the AI in different ways and reasonably expect different results (e.g., "check the math of all the equations" vs "detect all typos").

Novelty and impact

It's best to use AI to find related work, not to decide whether a paper is new. A model can search the literature, turn up earlier work the authors didn't cite and show you where the paper sits among its neighbors. Just check that every paper it names actually exists.

What it can't tell you is whether the work matters. But people are bad at this too. In 2014, NeurIPS sent 170 papers to two independent committees. Of the papers one committee accepted, the other accepted only about half. Cortes and Lawrence concluded that the process was good at spotting poor papers and poor at spotting good ones.4

Novel work is the hardest to judge. In a randomized experiment with research grants, experts gave lower scores to more novel proposals.5 The most novel papers are more likely to become big hits but also more likely to flop and they take years longer to be recognized.6 So a reviewer who can't tell whether a new idea will matter isn't failing. Nobody can tell reliably. You still have to make the call, so base it on what the search finds and treat it as one part of your broader assessment.

How long these results last

Everything above is about particular models at a particular time. Queen and Zou retested 50 well-known findings about AI models on 20 recent models. Most no longer held or had gotten much weaker within about 15 months (Figure 5).7 The failures that faded were mostly mistakes with a right answer, like making up citations, which developers can measure and train away. The ones that still last are about judgment: models still score accepted and rejected papers about the same, still prefer their own answers and still flatter the user. Checking keeps getting better and judging hasn't been able to keep up. At the same time, judging itself is harder to evaluate.

Pre-screening a paper

Where does this leave us?

Before a full review, it's worth a quick pass for problems that don't need judgment. This is where AI is strongest and some conferences, like ICLR 2026, already do it.1 A good screen asks three things:

  • Do the numbers agree? Compare the abstract, the text, the tables and the figures.
  • Does the math hold up? Run a checker on the equations and derivations, then verify each report.
  • Is the paper complete? Look for missing sections, or results the abstract promises and the paper never shows.

Keep the screen to facts. Don't ask for a summary, a score or an opinion yet, because that would shape how you read the paper.

If you're an author, run the same screen on your own paper before you submit. It costs almost nothing2 and it's better that you find the mistakes than a reviewer does.

How to work

Different papers need different kinds of review. In a theory paper, you would check the proofs. In a paper about an agent system, the code, the setup and the evaluation matter more than the equations.

  1. Run the screen, then read the paper yourself and decide what you think before you see any model's opinion.
  2. Ask for checks on the paper. Have it look for wrong equations, numbers that don't match, claims without support and suspect references. Ask it to quote the passage for each.
  3. Check every report yourself. Nothing is a mistake until you've read the passage and agree.
  4. Look for what it missed, especially in arguments made in prose.
  5. Write the verdict yourself. Novelty, importance and soundness are your call.

Reviewing ethically

The authors sent their paper to you, not to the software. That has a few consequences.

First, most of the time the paper is confidential. The US National Institutes of Health bans its reviewers from using generative AI on grant applications for this reason.8 At a Committee on Publication Ethics forum, sharing a manuscript with a "non-proprietary AI" was called a breach of confidentiality.9 Only use tools your venue has approved. If you're not sure, don't.

Second, don't sign anything you don't believe. Your name is on the review, not the model's. If you haven't confirmed a finding, or you don't actually hold an opinion, leave it out.

Third, judge the science, not the writing. AI scores go up when a model polishes the prose.1 If you lean on them, you're rewarding papers a model polished and punishing the ones it didn't.

Fourth, follow your venue's rules, because they differ a lot. ICLR 2026 lets reviewers use AI to help write reviews. NeurIPS 2025 banned it for core reviewing tasks and ICML 2026 let authors choose.1 Read the policy before you open any tool.
